Software Identity Binding Unique Identifier

An identity names an actor. A binding establishes what that actor currently is.

The durable problem

Software is increasingly expected to act, authenticate, receive authority, invoke resources, produce evidence, and operate across environments without continuous human supervision.

In that environment, an identifier alone answers only part of the question. It may tell a system which software identity is being presented. It does not, by itself, establish what currently stands behind that identity.

A software identity may outlive a key. A key may outlive a build. A build may move between runtimes. Authority may be delegated, narrowed, renewed, or withdrawn. Evidence may be refreshed while the named actor remains operationally continuous.

How do we identify the binding between a software identity and the state, evidence, and authority that make that identity meaningful?

What SIBUID names

SIBUID is a descriptive term for a unique identifier used to distinguish a software-identity binding.

The term does not prescribe a protocol, identifier syntax, credential format, registry, or trust architecture. Its role is semantic: to identify the binding being referred to, rather than merely the software actor or an individual piece of evidence about it.

Core boundary

A SIBUID identifies a binding as delimited by a continuity policy. Whether that SIBUID persists across an authorized change, or a new one is assigned, is a policy question. This reference does not impose one universal answer; it names the question and the object to which the answer applies.

Identity, binding, snapshot

Software identity

Who or what is being identified.

Software-identity binding

The defined relationship between that identity and the properties, state, evidence, or authority that give it operational meaning.

SIBUID

The unique identifier assigned to that binding within the continuity boundary chosen by the system.

Snapshot identifier

An identifier or digest for one exact recorded state, statement, attestation, or evidence object.

Why the problem grows

As software becomes more autonomous and mobile, the distance between the name of the actor and the state actually represented by that name becomes more operationally important.

A service, workload, container, scheduled function, device-adjacent software process, or AI agent can retain a stable identity while keys, builds, runtimes, authority, and evidence change around it.

The mechanisms used to establish software identity will evolve. The underlying distinction is more durable:

The actor is one thing; the binding that tells us what that actor currently represents is another.

Scope

This reference does not claim a new Internet protocol or standards-track identifier. It does not replace authentication, authorization, workload identity, attestation, transparency, or provenance systems.

It names a semantic role that may be implemented using existing or future mechanisms.
